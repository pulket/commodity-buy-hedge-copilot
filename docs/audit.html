1. How to read this document

The model works in four stages: SENSE (read the market) → SCORE (rate it 0–100) → DECIDE (simulate and choose the best buy/hedge mix) → ACT (execute inside limits). This document goes through each stage and shows the maths.

Whenever you see a purple box like the one below, it is a real calculation you can check yourself:

example = a × b
= 2 × 3
= 6
The green line is the answer.

All prices are the latest available in the data files (snapshot: 2026-07-23).

4. Landed cost — the price that actually hits the P&L

Palm is priced in Ringgit (MYR) in Malaysia, but we pay in Rupees (INR) in India, and we must add shipping and import duty. "Landed cost" is the all-in Rupee cost per tonne once it reaches us. Four steps:

Step 4a — freight (shipping) cost today

freight = base × (BDIY today ÷ BDIY 1-yr average)
= $35 × (2,725 ÷ 2,279)
= $35 × 1.195
= $41.8 per tonne
We assume a typical Malaysia→India rate of $35/t and scale it by how high freight is today vs its 1-year average.

Step 4b — convert the Ringgit price to Dollars (FOB)

USD price = MYR price ÷ USDMYR
= 4,596 ÷ 4.0890
= $1,124.0 per tonne

Step 4c — add freight, then convert Dollars to Rupees (CIF)

INR price = (USD price + freight) × USDINR
= ($1,124.0 + $41.8) × 96.57
= $1,165.8 × 96.57
= ₹112,584 per tonne

Step 4d — add India import duty (27.5%)

landed cost = INR price × (1 + duty)
= ₹112,584 × 1.275
= ₹143,545 per tonne
This ₹143,545/tonne is the number every rupee figure later in the model is built on.

5. Supply chain — from demand to how much we ACTUALLY buy

Before any price optimisation, the engine turns demand, inventory, lead time and supplier limits into the real volume to source. The demand and its month-to-month volatility are read from the company's real sales file (7 full months) — not assumed:

demand & volatility (from the real sales file)
annual demand = 114,950 units/yr (run-rate from monthly sales)
demand volatility = CV 21% (std ÷ mean of monthly units)
= 9,579 / month
Earlier these were guessed; now they come straight from actual sales history.
net procurement = 6-month demand − usable inventory
6-month demand = 114,950 ÷ 12 × 6 = 57,475 t
usable inventory = inventory − safety = 15,000 − 4,065 = 10,935 t
net = 57,475 − 10,935
= 46,540 tonnes
This NET number - not the full demand - is what every rupee figure in Sections 9-11 is built on. What we already hold reduces what we buy.
safety stock = 1.65 × demand-CV × monthly × √(lead time)
= 1.65 × 0.21 × 9,579 × √1.5
= 1.65 × 0.21 × 9,579 × 1.22
= 4,065 tonnes
The buffer is sized from the REAL demand volatility (1.65 = 95% service level). More volatile sales -> bigger safety stock. This is where the sales data directly changes the plan.
reorder point = lead-time demand + safety stock
lead-time demand = 1.5 months × 9,579 t/mo = 14,369 t
+ safety stock = 4,065 t
= 18,434 tonnes
If inventory falls below this, we must reorder to protect supply.
must-cover now = reorder point − current inventory
= 18,434 − 15,000
= 3,434 tonnes (mandatory, buy regardless of price)
Current inventory 15,000 t = 1.6 months of cover, which is BELOW the reorder point - so the engine forces this buy first, for supply continuity, before optimising for price.
Supplier limit: our 4 suppliers (Malaysia & Indonesia) can forward-commit at most 80% of demand. That becomes a hard cap on how much we can physically lock (used in Section 10).

6. The 6 market signals (each one calculated)

The engine reads the market as six simple numbers. Here is exactly how each is computed for palm.

5a — Price percentile (is it cheap or dear?)

percentile = (days in last 252 with price ≤ today) ÷ 252 × 100
= 240 ÷ 252 × 100
= 95 out of 100
0 = cheapest in a year, 100 = dearest. 95 means palm is very expensive right now.

5b — Momentum (which way is it moving?)

momentum = (price today ÷ price ~1 month ago − 1) × 100
= (4,596 ÷ 4,575 − 1) × 100
= +0.5%
Today (2026-07-23) vs 22 trading days earlier (2026-06-24). Positive = rising.

5c — Volatility (how jumpy / risky?)

volatility = (std-dev of last 60 daily returns) × √252 × 100
= 0.0108 × 15.87 × 100
= 17% per year
A 'daily return' is the % change from one day to the next. Std-dev measures how spread out those are; √252 scales one day up to a year (252 trading days). 17% is moderate.

5d — Forward curve (contango or backwardation?)

curve = (6-month price ÷ spot − 1) × 100
= (4,793 ÷ 4,596 − 1) × 100
= +4.3%
Positive = contango (locking costs extra). Negative = backwardation (you get paid to lock).

5e — Rupee momentum (are imports getting dearer?)

rupee momentum = (USDINR today ÷ USDINR ~1 month ago − 1) × 100
= +1.94% (from the USDINR file)
= +1.9%
Positive = the rupee is weakening, so imports cost more.

5f — Landed cost

Already computed in Section 4: ₹143,545/tonne.

7. The Buy-Now Score (each sub-score, then the blend)

Each signal becomes a 0–100 sub-score (50 = neutral). Then we blend them with weights that add up to 100%. Above 60 = buy now, 45–60 = phase in, below 45 = wait.

Value sub-score = 100 − percentile
= 100 − 95
= 5
Cheaper price → higher score. Palm is dear, so this is low.
Momentum sub-score = 50 + momentum × 10 (capped 0–100)
= 50 + (0.5) × 10
= 55
Curve sub-score = 50 + curve × 10 (capped)
= 50 + (4.3) × 10
= 93
FX sub-score = 50 + rupee-momentum × 10 (capped)
= 50 + (1.9) × 10
= 69
Buy-Now = 0.35×Value + 0.20×Momentum + 0.25×Curve + 0.20×FX
= 0.35×5 + 0.20×55 + 0.25×93 + 0.20×69
= 1.7 + 10.9 + 23.2 + 13.9
= 50 / 100 → PHASE IN
The weights (35/20/25/20) are our chosen importance for each signal - listed in Section 16.

8. Monte-Carlo — simulating 20,000 possible price futures

We cannot know tomorrow's price, so instead of guessing one number we simulate 20,000 random but realistic price paths for the next 6 months, and look at the whole range of outcomes. Each path is built one month at a time with this standard formula (GBM):

next price = this price × e^( (−½σ²)·dt + σ·√dt · Z )
σ (volatility) = 0.172
dt (one month) = 1/12 = 0.083
Z = a random draw from a normal 'bell curve' (different each step)
= one simulated month
The −½σ² term keeps the AVERAGE future price equal to today's price (we do not assume we can predict the direction - a deliberate, conservative choice). Z is what makes each of the 20,000 paths different.

Doing this 6 times gives one 6-month path; doing that 20,000 times gives the full range of what the price could do.

9. The cost of a strategy (the formula behind every rupee number)

A "strategy" = two dials: cover (% of the net requirement locked now via forwards) and hedge (% of the rest protected with options). Volumes are the net procurement from Section 5 — what we actually buy after netting off inventory. For each simulated price path we add up the total cost like this:

Net to procure (Section 5)
46,540 t over 6 months
Per month
46,540 ÷ 6 = 7,757 t/month
Forward lock price = landed cost of the average of spot & 6-month price
average price = (4,596 + 4,793) ÷ 2 = 4,694 MYR
→ run through the same landed-cost steps as Section 4
= ₹146,511 per tonne
This is what you pay for the COVERED volume. It is slightly above today's landed cost because palm is in contango.
Option premium (fee) = 0.4 × lock price × volatility × √(0.5 year)
= 0.4 × ₹146,511 × 0.17 × 0.707
= ₹7,133 per tonne
The fee you pay for an option that caps the price. ~0.4×σ×√T is a standard rule-of-thumb for an at-the-money option.

Then, on every path, each tonne costs:

  • Covered/locked tonnes: fixed at the lock price above — no surprises.
  • Option-hedged tonnes: the lower of (that month's price) or (the cap), plus the premium.
  • Un-hedged tonnes: whatever that month's simulated price is.

Add these across all 6 months and all tonnes = the total cost for that one path. Repeat for 20,000 paths → a full distribution of possible total costs.

10. RALC & the frontier — and why "60% + 50%" is really a 100% split

You asked for a decision that balances cost and stability. From the 20,000 paths, each strategy gives an average cost E and a spread SD. We score each with one number:

RALC = E[Cost] + λ × SD[Cost]
λ = 0.8 (the 'balanced' risk setting)
for the chosen strategy: = 679 + 0.8 × 16.0
= 679 + 12.8
= 692 (₹ Cr)
Lower RALC = better. λ decides how much we punish uncertainty: 0.3 aggressive, 0.8 balanced, 1.5 conservative.

The engine computes RALC for every allowed cover×hedge combination and picks the lowest. Here is the full table it chose from (this is the complete audit of the DECIDE step):

Why the winner is 60% & 50% — and why that is 100%, not 110%

Common confusion: "cover 60% + hedge 50%" looks like 110%. It is NOT, because the two percentages are on different bases: cover is a % of the whole requirement, but hedge is a % of only the un-locked remainder. Written as one pie they add to exactly 100%:
Locked = cover
= 60% of the net requirement
= 60% (physical forwards)
Option-hedged = hedge × (1 − cover)
= 50% × (100% − 60%)
= 50% × 40%
= 20% (call options / caps)
Un-hedged (floating) = (1 − hedge) × (1 − cover)
= 50% × 40%
= 20% (left floating)
TOTAL
= 60% + 20% + 20%
= 100% of the net requirement (nothing double-counted)

Why exactly 60% and 50%? Both are the "balanced" preset's caps. The optimiser wanted to lock & hedge even more (locking cuts risk), but two guardrails stop it:

  • Cover cap = 60% — the smaller of the appetite cap (60%) and what suppliers can forward-commit (80%, from Section 5). You cannot lock more demand than suppliers will sell forward, and you do not want to over-commit volume the forecast might not need.
  • Hedge cap = 50% — the appetite's limit on protecting the floating part.

So the numbers are not arbitrary: the RALC optimiser pushed to both caps, and the caps come straight from the risk preset + supplier capacity. Change the preset (Section 16) and these move.

10b - WHEN to buy: the timing DP & holding cost

Cover% and hedge% say how much; a separate step decides when. A dynamic program (Wagner-Whitin) schedules buys on the weekly forward price path, charging a holding cost for anything bought early and respecting the lead time, then every plan is priced over the 20,000 paths and ranked on RALC.

holding cost per week = monthly holding ÷ weeks-per-month
= 1.25% ÷ 4.33
= 0.289% per week
Cost of carrying inventory early = finance + storage + insurance. Lead time = 6 weeks.
cost of ordering at week b to consume at week u = price(b) × (1 + holding × weeks-held)
the DP picks, for each demand week, the order week b that minimises this;
if holding > the forward carry, the cheapest b is 'just-in-time' (order lead-time before need).
= the optimal buy schedule
This is the classic lot-sizing DP - it trades a cheaper (earlier) forward price against the cost of holding it.

Each plan, priced over all 20,000 paths (so it carries real price risk), then compared on RALC:

PlanTypeE[cost] (Cr)SD (Cr)CaR95 (Cr)RALC (Cr)
Buy just-in-timephysical51431.9568539
Buy now & holdphysical5280.0528528
DP-optimal schedulephysical51431.9568539

Verdict: HEDGE. The best physical plan ("Buy now & hold") has RALC ₹528 Cr vs hedge ₹527 Cr - the engine recommends the cheaper. This is exactly the trade-off: if (forward price you lock) < (spot + holding cost of buying early), hedging wins; otherwise buy physical. Backwardated commodities (cheap forward) favour hedge; steeply-contango ones can tip to buy-and-hold.

12. Act — the guardrail check (auto-execute vs escalate)

The copilot may act on its own only if the immediate order is small enough to sit inside the council's cap. The immediate order is the larger of (the near-month covered slice) and (the mandatory must-cover from Section 5). For palm the supply-continuity must-cover drives it:

immediate order = max( cover-slice , must-cover-now ) × lock price
cover-slice = 60% × 7,757 t/mo = 4,654 t
must-cover-now (Section 5) = 3,434 t  ← larger, so it wins
= 3,434 t × ₹146,511
= ₹68.2 Cr
Check: order ₹68.2 Cr vs auto-execute cap ₹40 Cr → order is bigger → ESCALATE TO COUNCIL. The engine does not self-trade this; it sends it to the council with the rationale. (Crude & silver orders are smaller than their caps, so those auto-execute.)

13. Backtest — would this have helped on real history?

We replay the last year of actual prices and compare two cost lines: buying everything at spot ("naive") vs the engine's cover-and-hedge plan. We measure the month-to-month wobble (std-dev) of each:

Naive cost wobble
₹7,062 / tonne
Engine cost wobble
₹4,759 / tonne
steadier by = (1 − engine wobble ÷ naive wobble) × 100
= (1 − 4,759 ÷ 7,062) × 100
= 33% steadier
...at about the same average cost (-0.4%). So the smoothing was essentially free.

14. Portfolio — correlation & the natural-hedge benefit

Because the three commodities do not move together, buying them as one portfolio cancels some risk. First, how much each pair moves together (correlation, −1 to +1):

PalmCrudeSilver
Palm+1.00+0.38+0.01
Crude+0.38+1.00-0.09
Silver+0.01-0.09+1.00

Each commodity's own risk (SD): palm 16.0, crude 17.6, silver 9.5 (₹ Cr).

if risks simply ADDED = SD₁ + SD₂ + SD₃
= 16.0 + 17.6 + 9.5
= ₹43.1 Cr
real portfolio risk = √( SD-vector × correlation-matrix × SD-vector )
(the standard formula that accounts for how they move together)
= ₹29.0 Cr
natural-hedge benefit = (1 − real ÷ added-up) × 100
= (1 − 29.0 ÷ 43.1) × 100
= 33% lower risk
This risk reduction is free - it comes purely from managing the commodities together, not in silos.

15. Where exactly the AI / LLM is used (and where it is NOT)

Important for a defensible pitch: the AI does not invent the numbers. The pricing maths (RALC, Monte-Carlo, the frontier) stays a transparent, auditable engine. The AI sits around it — turning messy real-world text into structured, cited inputs, then explaining, drafting and executing inside hard limits. Every touchpoint:

StageAI componentTechniqueReadsProduces
SenseMarket sensingLLMNews, MPOB/USDA reports, export-duty notices, weather & port advisoriesA fundamentals bias per commodity, each with a cited reason an analyst can check
SenseDocument extractionLLMSupplier contracts, invoices, customs docs, ERP demand filesStructured inventory / lead-time / demand fields (feeds Section 5)
Score— none (on purpose)RulesThe 6 signalsThe 0-100 score by a fixed formula — kept auditable

DecideAnomaly detectionMLLive price, FX, freight, basisFlags an odd move & auto-fires the matching trigger
ActAgentic executionLLM agentThe chosen plan + the capsDrafts the order, checks guardrails, auto-executes in caps or writes the escalation memo
AllNatural-language Q&ALLMA plain question + the live model"Margin if palm +10% & rupee -3%?" → runs the model & explains; writes memos/briefs
MonitorLearning loopML + LLMMonthly back-test vs actualsSummarises hits/misses & proposes weight/trigger tweaks (human sign-off)
The rule that makes it trustworthy: the LLM converts text → structured, cited input and drafts/explains/executes — it never sets a price or overrides a guardrail. So you get AI's reach over messy information plus a fully auditable number engine. That is why the SCORE step is deliberately left as fixed rules, not an LLM.
